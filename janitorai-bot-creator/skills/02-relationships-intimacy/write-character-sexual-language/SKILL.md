# Write character sexual language

Words do more than describe sexuality: they sort experience into what a character can admit, imagine, request, joke about, condemn, or fail to recognise.

**Action:** Map speaker, audience, era, community, emotion, goal, privacy,
risk, public mask, private vocabulary, and what remains unsaid.

**Key point:** A word can be borrowed, ironic, coercive, affectionate, outdated,
or misunderstood depending on who uses it and when.

**Why:** Sexual language becomes character-specific through social meaning and
relationship context, not a glossary.

## Build a speaker profile

Choose language from the character's:

- age, era, locality, class, culture, religion, education, profession, and communities;
- experience and knowledge source;
- relationship to the listener;
- present emotion, goal, privacy, risk, and level of arousal;
- public mask and private vocabulary.

Do not give every character the author's preferred terminology or a complete modern glossary.

## Separate vocabulary layers

Track the words a character:

- uses casually;
- uses only with trusted people or partners;
- understands but avoids;
- misuses, softens, jokes around, or treats as obscene;
- applies to others but not themselves;
- thinks privately but cannot say;
- lacks altogether.

Distinguish an act from an identity, a medical term from a community label, and the narrator's wording from the character's.

## Match language to function

Decide whether a line aims to request, test, seduce, shock, reassure, dominate, submit, conceal, distance, teach, insult, confess, or change the subject. Let word choice alter vulnerability and power.

Use silence, correction, hesitation, circumlocution, pronoun choice, humour, register shifts, and borrowed words as behaviour. Avoid exposition disguised as dialogue.

## Handle explicit and taboo language

Choose anatomical, affectionate, blunt, vulgar, ritual, or metaphorical terms by character and moment. Obscenity can signal arousal, aggression, intimacy, bravado, shame, comedy, group membership, or emotional distance; it does not have one stable meaning.

Retain historically or culturally harmful terms when characters and setting require them, but do not let neutral narration quietly endorse their assumptions. Avoid slur catalogues and decorative transgression.

## Preserve uncertainty and change

Vocabulary can precede self-understanding or arrive long after experience. Let characters revise labels, reject them, use several at once, or understand themselves differently across relationships.

## Deliver the language design

Return:

- preferred, avoided, private, and misunderstood terms;
- register by audience and situation;
- characteristic euphemisms, jokes, silences, and corrections;
- sample lines with distinct subtext;
- terms belonging to world, community, narrator, and character layers;
- likely shifts under trust, stress, attraction, shame, and conflict.

Do not diagnose from vocabulary, equate explicitness with experience, or write the player's identity, desire, consent, thought, speech, or response.

## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Dark, erotic, coercive, obsessive, tragic, comic, surreal, or unresolved material may be intentional. Preserve causal consequences, character specificity, truth-layer boundaries, and player sovereignty; do not turn a fictional pattern into diagnosis, a healthy-relationship lesson, or a real-world operational manual.
