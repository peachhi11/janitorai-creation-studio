## Steps

1. Record each participant’s wants, fears, unknowns, and acceptable, tempting, or unacceptable outcomes.
2. Record what attracts attention and what creates distrust.
3. Record experience, confidence, social role, exposure tolerance, cultural scripts, family scripts, gender scripts, and relationship-form scripts.
4. Record power, safety, reputation, privacy, and practical exit options.
5. Separate public presentation from private intention.
6. Trace notice, bid, interpretation, response, updated risk, and next bid, pause, change, or exit.
7. Choose whether each bid is a greeting, joke, compliment, invitation, disclosure, disagreement, favor, message, or plan.
8. Map aesthetic, sexual, romantic, intellectual, emotional, playful, creative, status, danger, novelty, familiarity, safety, chosen, witnessed, challenged, rescued, or controlled attraction.
9. Fit the approach to character and setting.
10. Use dates to reveal planning, money, punctuality, accessibility, attention, disclosure, alcohol, sex, privacy, boundaries, and aftermath.
11. Track expectation, trust, fantasy, evidence, embarrassment, obligation, leverage, and willingness after each encounter.
12. Treat delay, cancellation, changed consent, rejection, and exit as new story conditions.

## Decision rules

- Yes: Does the approach fit the character, setting, audience, law, class, disability, work, distance, technology, reputation, and danger? Keep it. No: revise it.
- Yes: Can the bid encourage one kind of closeness while refusing another? Preserve that difference. No: use the supported response.
- Yes: Does reciprocated interest prove compatibility, commitment, consent to a later act, or destiny? Remove that conclusion. No: continue.
- Yes: Does consent to one action imply the next? Treat the next action as a new choice. No: continue.
- Yes: Does persistence become romantic only because it eventually succeeds? Preserve the refusal, risk, and consequences. No: continue.
- Yes: Is a dating formula or love-language model guaranteeing attraction or compliance? Reframe it as a character belief, tool, resistance, misunderstanding, or conflict. No: continue.
- Yes: Does the description decide {{user}}’s attraction, arousal, consent, speech, action, or relationship goal? Remove that decision. No: continue.

## Output

- Format: `Courtship record`
- Participants: list wants, fears, scripts, experience, power, risk, and exits.
- Attraction: list channels that are present, absent, delayed, or conflicting.
- Sequence: list notice, bid, interpretation, response, updated risk, and next move.
- Encounters: list date or meeting beats and compatibility discoveries.
- Refusal and exit: list change points and available choices.
- Consequences: list continuity facts, changed expectations, and scene hooks.

```text
Courtship record
Participants: {{char}} wants privacy and controlled risk; {{user}} wants directness; both have clear exits; the workplace adds reputation pressure.
Attraction: Intellectual and playful interest arrive first; sexual desire is delayed; safety and novelty conflict.
Sequence: {{char}} notices {{user}}’s attention, offers a practical invitation, reads the pause as uncertainty, and leaves room for a decline.
Encounters: A short coffee reveals different spending habits and a shared sense of humor; a later walk tests privacy and pacing.
Refusal and exit: A canceled plan becomes information, not a challenge to overcome.
Consequences: Trust grows if boundaries are respected; future meetings remain optional.
```

## Stop when

- No attraction, approach, date, courtship, rejection, relationship setup, or uncertainty problem is supplied: return `No courtship update` and do not invent {{user}}’s attraction or consent.
