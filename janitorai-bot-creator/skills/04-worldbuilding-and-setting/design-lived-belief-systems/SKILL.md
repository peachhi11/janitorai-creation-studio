# Design lived belief systems

Do NOT use this skill to treat one doctrine as every person's inner life or to impose compulsory virtue.

Build a belief system from what people do, inherit, argue over, hope for, fear, and organise—not only from propositions they claim to accept.

**Action:** Separate doctrine, practice, authority, dissent, material life, and supernatural truth, then show how different people inhabit them.
**Key point:** Belief is lived and contested rather than a single rulebook shared by a population.
**Why:** Internal variation creates credible pressure without flattening faith, doubt, ritual, or refusal.

## Set the truth boundary

Separate:

- what the fictional world establishes as true;
- what adherents commonly believe;
- official doctrine or authorised interpretation;
- local, household, folk, mystical, reform, and dissenting versions;
- what outsiders claim;
- what remains unknowable or disputed.

Decide whether gods, spirits, miracles, revelation, karma, fate, or an afterlife are confirmed, ambiguous, absent, or differently understood. Even confirmed supernatural facts do not force one interpretation.

## Build the tradition around questions

Choose the questions it helps people live with:

- Where does the world or community come from?
- What makes a life, action, person, place, or object meaningful?
- What is owed to gods, ancestors, strangers, kin, rulers, the dead, or future generations?
- Why do suffering, luck, injustice, death, and change occur?
- What can humans choose, and what lies beyond control?
- How may wrongdoing, impurity, debt, grief, or broken order be addressed?

Allow several answers to coexist. Do not convert metaphysical complexity into a simple good–evil alignment chart.

## Connect belief to practice

For each important claim, identify its lived expressions:

- prayer, meditation, oath, taboo, sacrifice, pilgrimage, divination, study, confession, mourning, feast, or refusal;
- household routines, clothing, food, timekeeping, names, architecture, art, burial, care, and hospitality;
- specialists, training, texts, oral transmission, relics, sacred places, and material supplies;
- costs in time, money, labour, risk, status, or obligation;
- exceptions for emergency, rank, age, disability, travel, mixed households, or competing duties.

People may participate from conviction, habit, belonging, fear, duty, pleasure, politics, or necessity.

## Design authority and interpretation

Map who may teach, interpret, lead rites, declare error, grant exceptions, control property, or speak for the tradition. Include formal and informal authority: clergy, scholars, elders, visionaries, patrons, rulers, families, popular performers, and local experts.

Ask how disagreements are handled. Schism, commentary, syncretism, reform, persecution, accommodation, indifference, and quiet local variation create different stories. Do not make every religious institution corrupt or every dissenter enlightened.

## Place belief in history and society

Trace contact with conquest, migration, trade, state power, class, gender, language, education, science, technology, and other traditions. Beliefs can legitimate power, constrain it, offer refuge, organise care, preserve memory, intensify conflict, or do several at once.

Distinguish official religion from vernacular practice without treating the latter as a degraded copy. Track who benefits, who is burdened, and who can safely refuse.

## Make it usable in fiction

Create scene-level evidence:

- a calendar interruption or ritual preparation;
- a phrase used sincerely by one person and ironically by another;
- a dispute over obligation, interpretation, or authority;
- an object whose value depends on provenance and use;
- a rule applied differently in public and private;
- a moment when doctrine does not supply an easy answer.

Reveal belief through purpose-driven speech and action. Characters should not sound like comparative-religion lecturers unless that is genuinely their role.

## Audit the system

- Avoid one doctrine, leader, or text standing for every adherent.
- Do not equate religion with irrationality or secularism with neutrality.
- Do not make all ethics depend on divine command, or all religious ethics identical.
- Keep supernatural truth, institutional power, personal faith, and moral worth distinct.
- Preserve coercive, fanatical, hypocritical, compassionate, ordinary, ecstatic, doubting, and politically compromised possibilities.
- For roleplay, describe social and supernatural pressures without dictating a player's belief, devotion, doubt, consent, or choice.

## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Preserve the setting's internal rules, causal consequences, knowledge boundaries, and player sovereignty. Do not turn fictional darkness, eroticism, coercion, obsession, tragedy, comedy, surrealism, or meta-fiction into a real-world manual or an automatic healthy outcome.
