# Build story-driven worlds

Build outward from use. A setting earns detail when it changes what someone can notice, want, risk, choose, or do.

**Action:** Define who uses the world, what they want, which setting element
changes the choice, and what can remain open.

**Key point:** Build only enough setting to create meaningful consequences; do
not treat completeness as quality.

**Why:** Story-driven scope protects attention and keeps worldbuilding attached
to character motion.

## Find the working centre

Write a short brief that answers:

1. Who will experience this world, and from what position?
2. What are they trying to do?
3. What part of the world helps, obstructs, tempts, or misleads them?
4. What mood and genre promises should the setting support?
5. Which facts must be settled now, and which can remain open?

If the story is still loose, begin with one charged place, relationship, institution, custom, resource, or disruption. Expand only far enough to create useful choices and consequences.

## Build the minimum useful setting

For each planned scene or recurring interaction, identify:

- where it happens and who controls access;
- what ordinary people expect there;
- what the viewpoint character knows, assumes, or gets wrong;
- what resources, rules, dangers, and opportunities affect action;
- which social differences matter in practice;
- what may change because of the characters.

Answer the questions that affect play or prose. Park interesting but unused material in a later list rather than forcing it into the draft.

## Connect the pieces

Do not design geography, law, religion, magic, technology, work, kinship, or class as separate forms. Link each important element to at least two others.

Names are part of those links. Use distinct personal, house, title, and place
categories when naming a culture or institution; record whether a form comes
from the core culture, a class or role, or a frontier contact layer. Place names
can preserve older linguistic strata after the people, rulers, or language of a
region have changed. See `design-layered-naming-systems` when the naming logic
needs its own audit.

For example, a dangerous winter may change food storage, travel, household size, seasonal work, hospitality, military timing, and the political value of warm ports. Choose the links that matter to this story; do not complete every possible branch.

Establish an ordinary baseline before emphasising the strange. Readers understand an exception more readily when they can see what it interrupts.

## Make the world produce story

Use setting elements to create:

- goals that make sense in local terms;
- obstacles with more than one possible response;
- rewards whose value depends on the recipient and community;
- obligations, debts, privileges, and unwanted attention;
- disagreements between law, custom, belief, and practice;
- consequences that can alter later scenes.

Rules should create intelligible pressure, not predetermine the protagonist or player character's response. Tell players what their characters would reasonably know before asking for a consequential decision.

## Trace social pressure through a scene

For any institution, custom, status marker, resource, or taboo that matters to
the story, trace:

`rule or expectation → access or burden → character tactic → witness or gatekeeper → immediate consequence → later opportunity or constraint`

This keeps worldbuilding connected to lived choices. A rule may be evaded,
weaponised, misread, selectively enforced, or privately rejected. Record who
benefits, who pays the labour or risk, and what informal support or resistance
exists. Do not make the setting's official values the narrator's values, and do
not decide how the player character interprets or responds to the pressure.

## Control scope

Expand when a new scene, character, decision, or contradiction requires it. Stop when the available detail supports the next meaningful stretch of story.

Use questionnaires as menus. Skip prompts that do not affect the work. A blank answer is not a flaw, and a completed template is not proof that a setting feels alive.

Treat worldbuilding checklists and web guides as menus, not as universal laws.
Avoid a snow-globe setting in which every culture, economy, belief, and
relationship follows one neat pattern. Give major systems internal variation,
contact history, and at least one ordinary consequence; leave low-value detail
open for discovery.

For publication or handoff, test for minimum viable coherence rather than
completion: a stranger should be able to orient themselves, understand the
setting's promise, and see why the important rules matter without the author
present to explain every gap. Keep private canon, player-facing material, and
open mysteries distinct.

Use a purpose gate before expanding a setting: name the scene, decision,
audience, or player need this detail serves. If a fact cannot yet change
action, interpretation, atmosphere, access, or future possibility, park it as
optional lore rather than allowing the setting to become an encyclopaedia.

Apply the same test to realistic, contemporary, historical, romance, and
literary fiction. Worldbuilding includes social context, institutions,
relationships, work, language, memory, and material routines—not only maps,
magic, or invented history.

## Audit the setting

Check that:

- the most developed material will appear in decisions, relationships, or consequences;
- institutions have people, labour, resources, and interests behind them;
- cultures contain regional, generational, class, occupational, and individual variation;
- peoples or species are not assigned one shared temperament or morality;
- genre and tone guide expectations without banning deliberate contrast;
- difficult, dark, or unequal worlds remain available without presenting their values as universal truth;
- player-authored motives, feelings, consent, and choices remain open;
- unresolved areas are marked as open rather than disguised with vague filler.

## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Preserve the setting's internal rules, causal consequences, knowledge boundaries, and player sovereignty. Do not turn fictional darkness, eroticism, coercion, obsession, tragedy, comedy, surrealism, or meta-fiction into a real-world manual or an automatic healthy outcome.
