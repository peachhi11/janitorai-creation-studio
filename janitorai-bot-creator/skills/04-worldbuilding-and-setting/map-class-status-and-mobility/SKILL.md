# Map class, status, and mobility

Do NOT use this skill to reduce people to a single class stereotype or to treat status as destiny.

Model stratification as unequal access across several dimensions. Wealth, office, ancestry, occupation, education, citizenship, gender, faith, reputation, legal standing, and control of force may reinforce one another or pull in different directions.

**Action:** Choose the dimensions that matter, map access and penalties, and trace how a character's movement changes relationships and options.
**Key point:** Status is relational and situational, not one universal ladder.
**Why:** Multiple forms of power make social consequences specific without turning class into caricature.

## Choose the relevant dimensions

Identify what people in this setting compete to obtain or protect:

- material security, property, credit, and income;
- legal rights, protection, and freedom of movement;
- political voice, office, and access to decision-makers;
- education, credentials, specialist knowledge, and respected work;
- honour, recognition, purity, lineage, fame, or belonging;
- control over other people's labour, time, bodies, or risk.

Use only the dimensions that affect the story. Do not compress them into one universal ladder.

## Map positions and relations

For each important group or position, record:

- what grants membership;
- what members can access that others cannot;
- what work, payment, obedience, or performance is expected;
- whom they depend on and who depends on them;
- which institutions protect or challenge their position;
- how insiders distinguish themselves in speech, dress, space, education, leisure, and association.

Include status inconsistency. A decorated officer may have little wealth; a rich trader may lack lineage; a revered healer may have weak legal standing. Such mismatches often create sharper conflict than a tidy hierarchy.

## Test reproduction

Ask how advantage and disadvantage persist across time:

- inheritance, marriage, kinship, and household strategy;
- property, debt, wages, rent, taxation, and unpaid work;
- schooling, apprenticeships, examinations, and credentials;
- law, policing, borders, guild rules, and restricted spaces;
- networks, recommendations, etiquette, accent, and presumed competence;
- stories that present the arrangement as natural, sacred, earned, or temporary.

Distinguish a system's justification from its operation. A society may praise merit while rewarding inherited preparation, tolerated failure, or private access.

## Design mobility as a route with costs

For upward, downward, intergenerational, and group-wide movement, specify:

1. the route;
2. who can attempt it;
3. the gatekeepers;
4. the visible price and hidden price;
5. the time required;
6. what old ties or obligations remain;
7. whether recognition follows material change.

Include blocked, partial, reversible, and contested mobility. One person's advancement may rely on another person's labour or displacement. Large political, environmental, or economic changes can move whole groups without reflecting individual merit.

## Add internal variation and coalition

Classes and ranked groups contain factions, rival interests, and unequal members. Show alliances that cross one divide while preserving another: employers and skilled workers protecting a trade, impoverished nobles defending lineage, or outsiders joining officials against a local monopoly.

Do not assign a single personality, morality, opinion, or culture to a social position. Position shapes pressures and options; it does not write the character.

## Convert the map into story evidence

Express rank through access and consequence:

- who waits, enters, speaks first, receives privacy, or is believed;
- who can make a mistake without lasting damage;
- whose time is treated as disposable;
- which goods are affordable, respectable, licensed, or forbidden;
- who can call on protection, credit, witnesses, education, or escape;
- what changes when a character crosses a boundary.

Keep player-authored feelings and choices open. Explain what a character would reasonably know about the risks without deciding whether they feel shame, loyalty, ambition, attraction, or resentment.

## Audit the system

- Avoid a single pyramid when several kinds of standing exist.
- Do not present caste, class, race, gender, faith, or occupation as biological destiny.
- Do not treat open systems or meritocracy as descriptions of equal opportunity without testing access and inheritance.
- Distinguish endogamy, exogamy, arranged marriage, preference, law, and practical constraint accurately.
- Preserve exceptions without using one exceptional character to prove the system is fair.
- Let status change alter relationships, obligations, and self-presentation rather than merely changing a label.

## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Preserve the setting's internal rules, causal consequences, knowledge boundaries, and player sovereignty. Do not turn fictional darkness, eroticism, coercion, obsession, tragedy, comedy, surrealism, or meta-fiction into a real-world manual or an automatic healthy outcome.
