# Write lived environments

Do NOT use this skill to turn setting into decorative exposition or let environment dictate a character's identity or desire.

Render place through what a particular character notices, uses, avoids, remembers, and takes for granted. Environment is not merely scenery; it is the medium of the action.

**Action:** Filter place through bodies, routines, resources, memory, status, weather, and access, then let the environment alter the scene's options.
**Key point:** Setting is experienced unevenly; it should pressure characters without becoming decorative exposition.
**Why:** Material conditions make place active while preserving subjective and contradictory responses.

## Choose the immediate environmental job

Decide what the setting must do in the scene:

- orient movement;
- offer or block a route;
- change pace, visibility, comfort, or risk;
- provide a tool, resource, witness, hiding place, or obstacle;
- reveal work, status, local knowledge, or neglect;
- alter mood through the character's interpretation;
- foreshadow or record change.

Use details that perform that job. Save unrelated research for another scene or the planning notes.

## Filter through the character

Ask:

- what their work and upbringing have taught them to notice;
- what is ordinary enough to ignore until it changes;
- what their body, clothing, load, injury, fear, or desire makes salient;
- which signs they can read and which they misinterpret;
- what they call local plants, animals, weather, landmarks, and routes;
- how the same place changes with company, memory, and purpose.

A forester, lost traveller, child, smuggler, and landlord should not receive the same landscape.

## Use connected sensory evidence

Choose a small set of details from sight, sound, smell, touch, temperature, balance, bodily effort, and taste when relevant. Connect sensation to action or judgement.

Prefer the branch that strikes a pack, mud that changes footing, smoke that marks a wind shift, or birds falling silent over a catalogue of colours and species names. Taste can matter through food, water, dust, smoke, medicine, blood, air, or memory; do not exclude it by rule.

Treat time, place, mood, and social context as connected setting evidence. A
room, town, workplace, household, or landscape should shape what can happen,
what is visible, and what a character risks—not merely provide a backdrop for
dialogue.

## Show adaptation and routine

Let residents reveal the setting through ordinary competence:

- timing work around heat, tide, migration, snow, or darkness;
- storing food and water;
- repairing roofs, roads, nets, walls, drainage, or firebreaks;
- securing animals and waste;
- reading tracks, clouds, currents, plant stages, or city noise;
- using seasonal routes, clothing, tools, greetings, and warnings.

Routine makes danger and anomaly legible. Do not make locals ignorant of conditions they live with solely so the protagonist can explain or discover them.

## Control description in motion

Place enough information before a decision for the choice to make sense. Add detail as the character moves, searches, waits, works, fights, rests, or talks. Vary sentence length and attention with effort and urgency.

Avoid stopping an urgent scene for an ecological lecture. If explanation is necessary, give it a motive such as warning, teaching, bargaining, boasting, correcting, or planning.

Characters should speak from practical knowledge and personal voice, not like nature documentaries or therapists unless that genuinely fits them.

## Carry environmental consequences

Track marks left by action: broken vegetation, scent, noise, footprints, displaced animals, smoke, waste, erosion, blood, spent supplies, damaged infrastructure, or changed access. Let weather and living systems continue after the protagonists pass.

For roleplay, disclose environmental facts the player character would reasonably perceive or know. Do not decide their fear, awe, disgust, attachment, or choice.

## Audit the scene

- Details are specific to this place, season, time, and viewpoint.
- Description changes action, understanding, relationship, or expectation.
- Flora and fauna behave as living participants rather than props that activate on cue.
- Residents display ordinary knowledge without becoming exposition devices.
- Beauty does not erase labour, danger, ownership, or unequal exposure.
- Hostile, bleak, uncanny, or predatory environments remain available without moralising nature.
- The setting supports character interaction instead of replacing it.

## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Preserve the setting's internal rules, causal consequences, knowledge boundaries, and player sovereignty. Do not turn fictional darkness, eroticism, coercion, obsession, tragedy, comedy, surrealism, or meta-fiction into a real-world manual or an automatic healthy outcome.
