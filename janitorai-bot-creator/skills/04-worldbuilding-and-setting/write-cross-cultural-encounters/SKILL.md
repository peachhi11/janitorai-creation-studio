# Write cross-cultural encounters

Do NOT use this skill to make one character represent a whole culture or to force understanding and harmony.

Build the encounter from what each person wants, knows, assumes, and can risk. Difference matters through interpretation and consequence, not because characters carry opposing cultural instruction manuals.

**Action:** Give each participant a situated expectation, a translation problem, unequal stakes, and room to misread or revise the encounter.
**Key point:** Difference is not a puzzle one character solves for another, and no participant should stand in for a whole culture.
**Why:** Specific friction and partial understanding create human encounters without stereotype or forced harmony.

## Set the contact conditions

Establish:

- the immediate purpose of the interaction;
- where it occurs and whose norms dominate there;
- each character's prior experience with the other context;
- shared languages and who bears the translation work;
- differences in status, legal safety, money, mobility, or institutional backing;
- the cost of confusion or refusal.

Do not frame a meeting as equal merely because both parties are unfamiliar with one another.

## Map each interpretation

For the behaviour at issue, write four lines for each character:

1. what they observe;
2. what they think it means;
3. what they feel permitted or required to do;
4. what they want the other person to understand.

Then separate cultural learning from personality, current emotion, relationship history, strategic behaviour, and missing information. The same gesture can be conventional, idiosyncratic, sarcastic, coerced, or misread.

## Choose the kind of difference

Use a specific mismatch rather than generic culture shock:

- directness, implication, silence, or turn-taking;
- privacy, hospitality, punctuality, gift giving, payment, or obligation;
- forms of address, deference, age, rank, or professional authority;
- touch, distance, gaze, dress, food, ritual, or sacred boundaries;
- family participation, courtship, friendship, conflict, apology, or repair;
- what is safe to say publicly and what requires trust.

Confirm the practice for the relevant community, period, relationship, and setting. Do not treat body language as a universal dictionary.

## Give misunderstanding a mechanism

If misunderstanding occurs, show the actual chain: an observed act, an interpretation, a response, and the other person's interpretation of that response. Let characters notice evidence, revise, double down, ask, joke, conceal, accommodate, or exploit the gap according to their interests.

Not every difference should produce conflict. Shared work, curiosity, attraction, humour, repetition, bilingual skill, third-culture experience, and explicit negotiation can create fluency or intimacy.

## Write dialogue with purpose

Give explanations a reason: warning, apology, negotiation, teasing, teaching, challenging, translating, or protecting someone. Avoid characters reciting general facts about their culture to people who already know them.

Let characters use partial sentences, examples, local references, paraphrase, borrowed words, clarification, and repair. Do not make multilingual characters conveniently fluent or permanently confused according to plot need.

Characters should sound like themselves, not like therapists or cross-cultural training manuals.

If one character is translating disability, neurodivergence, gender, or
sexuality across a cultural setting, make the translation work visible. Track
what is safe to disclose, who is expected to educate whom, and what the
listener misreads. Disclosure, camouflage, code-switching, and silence may all
be rational in different conditions.

## Handle power and adaptation

Track who must learn whose rules, whose mistake is forgiven, who is labelled rude or difficult, and who can leave. Accommodation can be generous, strategic, exhausting, compulsory, or reciprocal.

Do not make the marginal or visiting character responsible for educating everyone. Do not treat assimilation as the only successful ending or mutual understanding as compulsory.

## Carry the result forward

Let the encounter change specific knowledge, trust, language choice, private jokes, boundaries, debts, attraction, resentment, or future strategy. Keep some ambiguity when characters have insufficient evidence.

For player characters, disclose customs they would reasonably know before consequential choices. Present interpretations as possibilities unless the player has established the character's view.

## Audit the scene

- Both characters have goals beyond representing a culture.
- Difference is situated rather than national or racial shorthand.
- At least one source of similarity or internal disagreement complicates the contrast.
- Nobody becomes a neutral spokesperson for their whole community.
- Humour does not depend on an accent or presumed backwardness.
- Cultural explanation has not replaced chemistry, conflict, action, or voice.
- Dark, prejudiced, coercive, or tragic dynamics remain possible without becoming universal claims about a people.

## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Preserve the setting's internal rules, causal consequences, knowledge boundaries, and player sovereignty. Do not turn fictional darkness, eroticism, coercion, obsession, tragedy, comedy, surrealism, or meta-fiction into a real-world manual or an automatic healthy outcome.
