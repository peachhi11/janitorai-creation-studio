# Design story-useful geography

Build the part of the world the story can reach. Geography should shape access, time, work, danger, resources, and relationships without determining a society's culture or a character's personality.

**Action:** Set scale, purpose, terrain, water, climate, routes, resources,
hazards, and the choices they change.

**Key point:** Physical constraints create pressure; they do not dictate a
people's culture or a character's destiny.

**Why:** Story-useful geography keeps maps attached to travel, labour,
relationships, and scene consequence.

## Set scale and purpose

Choose the working scale: room and street, settlement, valley, region, continent, planet, or travel corridor. Note:

- the viewpoint and story area;
- locations characters must reach;
- resources, hazards, borders, and routes that affect action;
- the degree of realism or deliberate impossibility;
- what must stay open for discovery.

Use a rough map as a continuity tool. North need not be at the top, and the map need not be publication-ready.

## Build terrain and water together

Place major elevation, coasts, basins, and drainage before fine detail. Ask:

- where water comes from, collects, flows, freezes, floods, evaporates, or disappears underground;
- which divides separate watersheds;
- where crossings, passes, harbours, wells, springs, wetlands, and defensible sites occur;
- what erosion, deposition, tides, ice, volcanism, or deliberate engineering changes;
- where enclosed basins or unusual magical processes break familiar patterns.

Rivers generally join and follow gradients, but they need not all reach an ocean. Check real analogues when physical accuracy matters.

## Connect climate to place

Choose broad controls appropriate to the scale: latitude, elevation, distance from large water bodies, currents, prevailing winds, topography, seasonal cycles, and any invented forces.

Trace their combined effects rather than assigning climate from one feature. A mountain range can create wetter and drier sides when moist air crosses it; orientation alone does not create a rain shadow.

Record variation and extremes as well as averages. Storms, droughts, fire seasons, snowmelt, monsoons, or magical cycles matter when they alter decisions.

## Place movement and settlement

People often settle where they can obtain water, food, shelter, transport, trade, security, or sacred and political value. They also remain in difficult places through history, attachment, coercion, investment, or lack of alternatives.

For each route, estimate:

- distance and terrain;
- mode of travel and sustainable pace;
- weather, season, visibility, rest, maintenance, and load;
- crossings, bottlenecks, tolls, borders, safe stops, and detours;
- who can use the route and who cannot.

Maintain a simple journey calendar. Do not calculate false precision when pace depends on unstable conditions.

When naming settlements, routes, rivers, peaks, or borders, treat names as
historical evidence as well as labels. Features may retain older linguistic
strata while settlements and fortifications acquire later names through trade,
conquest, administration, or migration. Keep those layers distinct and make
the pronunciation usable aloud; use `design-layered-naming-systems` for the
full naming-system workflow.

## Make geography generate choices

Use physical relationships to create several options: a fast exposed pass, a slow supplied road, a seasonal river route, a guarded bridge, or a local path outsiders miss. Avoid terrain that exists only to force one plot outcome.

Connect geography to work, housing, food, infrastructure, warfare, migration, health, and political leverage through causal steps. Do not claim that environment automatically produces one culture or moral character.

## Audit continuity

Check that:

- distances, directions, travel times, and sightlines agree across scenes;
- water and elevation relationships are intelligible;
- weather and seasons affect clothing, light, roads, food, and activity consistently;
- settlements have plausible reasons to exist and means to obtain necessities;
- hazards affect residents as well as visiting protagonists;
- maps reflect story truth but leave room for inaccurate local knowledge, disputed borders, and unmapped places;
- magical exceptions have consequences and remain exceptions for a reason.

## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Preserve the setting's internal rules, causal consequences, knowledge boundaries, and player sovereignty. Do not turn fictional darkness, eroticism, coercion, obsession, tragedy, comedy, surrealism, or meta-fiction into a real-world manual or an automatic healthy outcome.
