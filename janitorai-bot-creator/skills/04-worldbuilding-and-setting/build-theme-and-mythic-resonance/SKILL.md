# Build theme and mythic resonance

Treat theme as a contested question embodied by characters, choices, images, institutions, and consequences. Do not reduce it to a lesson the narrative must prove.

**Action:** Frame a live thematic question, give characters situated and conflicting answers, and let recurring images gain meaning through consequences.
**Key point:** Theme can remain unresolved, tragic, erotic, or morally uncomfortable when that is the work's design.
**Why:** A contested pattern supports character agency instead of forcing an authorial verdict or redemption arc.

## Build the thematic structure

1. State the thematic tension as a question with at least two defensible answers.
2. Assign each important character a situated answer shaped by experience, desire, power, and blind spots. Allow behaviour to contradict professed belief.
3. Identify the costs each answer creates for the character and for other people. Keep responsibility proportionate; suffering does not erase agency or harm.
4. Choose two or three recurring concrete carriers: an object, setting, bodily action, ritual, social rule, or sensory detail. Change their meaning through context rather than explaining them repeatedly.
5. Put the theme under pressure through decisions. Let consequences reveal the argument; do not make dialogue deliver an authorial verdict unless that voice belongs to a character.
6. Leave room for unresolved tension, tragic outcomes, corrupt choices, and incompatible goods when the requested fiction calls for them.

## Use mythic material as a pattern library

Select a pattern because it sharpens the story's existing question, not because it is supposedly universal. Useful families include:

- creation, inheritance, and contested order;
- gods or leaders who embody and legitimise power;
- exceptional figures whose gifts carry obligations or exclusions;
- monsters produced by, excluded from, or opposed to an order;
- animal transformation, kinship, appetite, or mediation;
- underworld descent, grief, retrieval, and irreversible knowledge;
- journeys, trials, return, and failed return;
- judgement, afterlife, memory, and social accounting;
- destruction, recurrence, survival, and remaking.

Translate the selected pattern into story-specific roles and pressures. A descent may be social, psychological, political, literal, or several at once. Do not force a hero's journey, redemption arc, or restoration of order.

## Ground cultural use

- Identify the culture, period, community, and transmission route of borrowed material.
- Distinguish a living religious or cultural practice from a freely circulating generic motif.
- Check whether a familiar summary was written from a colonial, elite, outsider, or outdated scholarly viewpoint.
- Avoid merging distinct traditions because they share a surface feature.
- Preserve local contradictions and variations instead of treating one version as canonical.
- When evidence is thin, describe the adaptation as an invention inspired by a source rather than an authentic representation.

## Choose an interpretive lens

Treat rationalist, etymological, allegorical, historical, naturalist, ritual, diffusionist, evolutionary, psychoanalytic, archetypal, structural, and historical-critical accounts as competing lenses. Do not present any single lens as settled fact.

Prefer a historical-critical stance for synthesis: examine the particular story, its setting, uses, variants, and transmission before applying broader explanations. Use psychoanalytic or archetypal readings as optional creative interpretations, never diagnoses or claims of inherited universal meaning.

## Protect interactive agency

- Place thematic pressure around player choices without deciding the player's beliefs, feelings, consent, or moral conclusion.
- Let non-player characters advocate, manipulate, misread, reward, or punish; keep those actions inside the fiction rather than treating them as system truth.
- Preserve knowledge boundaries. Symbols meaningful to the author or one faction are not automatically understood by every character.
- Do not reward only the morally approved choice unless the world and mechanics intentionally impose that bias and the user asked for it.

## Audit the result

Check that:

- the theme appears through distinct choices and consequences, not repeated explanation;
- characters hold recognisably different positions rather than acting as mouthpieces;
- motifs change or accumulate meaning;
- mythic parallels enrich rather than predetermine the plot;
- cultural specificity has not been replaced by claims of universality;
- darkness, coercion, obsession, tragedy, or moral ambiguity remain available when requested;
- player-authored interiority and decisions remain open;
- the narrative does not diagnose characters or excuse unequal harm through psychology or symbolism.

## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Preserve the setting's internal rules, causal consequences, knowledge boundaries, and player sovereignty. Do not turn fictional darkness, eroticism, coercion, obsession, tragedy, comedy, surrealism, or meta-fiction into a real-world manual or an automatic healthy outcome.
