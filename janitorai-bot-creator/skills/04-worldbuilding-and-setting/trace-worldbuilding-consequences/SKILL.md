# Trace worldbuilding consequences

Treat a setting premise as a change moving through a network, not as an isolated novelty. Follow only the branches that can affect the story.

**Action:** Define the change, scope, access, cost, unchanged conditions, and
the strongest three to five consequences.

**Key point:** Not every branch needs explanation; preserve unknowns and
contested accounts where the fiction benefits.

**Why:** Selective causality gives the world pressure without burying the
story under systems diagrams.

## Define the change

State the premise in concrete terms:

- What exists, happened, or works differently?
- Where and when does it apply?
- Who can access, control, resist, or misunderstand it?
- What does it cost in time, labour, material, status, danger, or obligation?
- What remains unchanged?

Separate firm setting facts from rumours, laws, ideals, propaganda, contested accounts, and deliberate unknowns.

## Follow the first consequences

Ask what the change directly alters in the relevant areas:

- survival, health, shelter, food, travel, and communication;
- work, skills, production, trade, ownership, and scarcity;
- family, intimacy, inheritance, education, and care;
- government, law, punishment, warfare, and borders;
- faith, ritual, taboo, art, language, and status;
- who gains choices, who loses them, and who bears the hidden work.

Choose the strongest three to five effects. Specific causal links are more useful than a complete inventory.

When the premise concerns intimacy, pleasure, taboo, or identity, trace it
through the same material network as law, work, money, kinship, belief,
ecology, and status. Do not isolate an “erotic culture” from housing,
inheritance, privacy, reputation, health, or institutional power. Keep private
practice and public script distinct.

For a genre blend or signature location, state the promise it makes and the
constraints it introduces. A castle, frontier, empire, portal, or science-
fantasy technology should have a strategic reason to exist and should alter
routes, resources, status, danger, or story scale. Do not borrow visual tropes
without their material and narrative consequences.

Run a second-order disruption check for transformative premises: if magic,
technology, climate, communication, or medicine changes, ask what happens to
work, trade, health, warfare, law, family, education, and ordinary household
practice. Preserve exceptions, uneven access, lag, workarounds, and unintended
effects; do not let one invention silently coexist with every old system.

## Follow the response

For each important effect, ask:

1. Who adapts first?
2. Who profits or gains authority?
3. Who resists, evades, imitates, or regulates it?
4. What new job, market, crime, ritual, institution, or dependency appears?
5. What workaround develops among people without formal access?
6. What unintended consequence follows from that response?

This second pass is often where a premise begins to feel inhabited rather than designed.

## Add variation and time

Do not give the whole world one response. Compare at least two positions shaped by region, wealth, occupation, age, faith, political interest, or personal history.

Trace change across a useful interval:

- immediate disruption;
- adaptation and argument;
- normalisation, backlash, or capture by powerful groups;
- remnants of the earlier order.

For alternate history, name the divergence, preserve forces that still exist, and avoid assuming a single change rewrites everything in one direction.

## Convert consequences into usable material

Turn the chain into evidence that can enter a scene:

- a price, queue, permit, tool, uniform, shortcut, repair, or shortage;
- a job someone depends on or despises;
- a courtesy, insult, superstition, joke, or euphemism;
- a privilege one character treats as ordinary;
- a dispute in which both sides want something concrete;
- a rule that can be obeyed, exploited, broken, or changed.

Let characters respond according to their own interests. A social consequence can constrain options without deciding anyone's thoughts or moral position.

## Test coherence without sanding away mystery

Check that costs do not vanish when inconvenient, access does not change solely to rescue the plot, and institutions react to repeated pressures. Track contradictions that would alter choices.

Keep uncertainty when it creates useful tension. Supernatural causes, old history, and disputed beliefs do not need a single confirmed explanation. Distinguish intentional ambiguity from an accidental contradiction.

## Avoid false universals

- Do not derive personality or morality from ancestry, species, nationality, or culture.
- Do not treat one household, class, faction, or historical account as the whole society.
- Do not import a real culture as decorative shorthand. Research its period, internal variation, and living significance.
- Do not assume technical capability guarantees equal access or social acceptance.
- Do not make oppression the only source of depth, or suffering a neutral aesthetic texture.

## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Preserve the setting's internal rules, causal consequences, knowledge boundaries, and player sovereignty. Do not turn fictional darkness, eroticism, coercion, obsession, tragedy, comedy, surrealism, or meta-fiction into a real-world manual or an automatic healthy outcome.
