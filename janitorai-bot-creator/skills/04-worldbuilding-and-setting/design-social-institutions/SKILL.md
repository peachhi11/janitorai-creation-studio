# Design social institutions

Treat an institution as a repeated way of organising relationships and work. Its public purpose, practical operation, and effect on different people may not match.

**Action:** Map need, labour, resources, roles, authority, beneficiaries, costs,
formal rules, routines, bargains, and disputed edges.

**Key point:** Institutions can sustain care, exploitation, legitimacy, resistance,
or contradiction at the same time.

**Why:** Institutional practice gives characters concrete constraints and
leverage.

## Set the scale and boundary

Name the part of society being designed: a household network, neighbourhood, profession, city, state, transnational body, or something else. Identify outside groups, dependencies, and overlapping authorities. Do not assume a country, culture, or institution has a clean edge.

## Define the institution

Answer six questions:

1. What recurring need, opportunity, threat, or claim does it address?
2. Who performs the essential work?
3. Who supplies land, money, materials, knowledge, legitimacy, or force?
4. Which roles exist, and what does each owe or expect from the others?
5. Who receives the benefits and who bears the costs?
6. What keeps the arrangement going when individual members leave?

Separate the institution's stated purpose from the interests it actually serves. A court may promise equal judgement while depending on fees, patronage, inaccessible language, or selective enforcement.

## Build roles as relationships

For every important role, record:

- entry: appointment, election, inheritance, purchase, examination, coercion, kinship, or informal recognition;
- authority: what the role may decide, demand, grant, or withhold;
- duties: expected work, care, payment, restraint, and accountability;
- dependencies: whose cooperation or labour makes the role possible;
- limits: law, custom, rivals, scarcity, reputation, conscience, or resistance;
- exit: resignation, removal, retirement, desertion, death, or no recognised route out.

Distinguish role expectation from role performance. Two people may occupy the same office differently, and one person may face conflict between several roles.

## Design formal and lived versions

Map four layers:

- formal rule: what charters, laws, doctrine, or policy say;
- working routine: what usually happens;
- informal bargain: favours, shortcuts, patronage, discretion, silence, or mutual aid;
- disputed edge: the case nobody agrees how to handle.

This gap creates room for competence, hypocrisy, reform, corruption, mercy, and conflict without making every institution secretly identical.

For schools, workplaces, healthcare, and support systems, map the access
labour: who must disclose, translate needs, fill forms, arrange transport,
prove eligibility, schedule around family duties, or absorb sensory and social
costs. “Individualised support” is a practice with staffing, time, discretion,
and limits, not a benevolent label.

## Test persistence and change

Ask what reproduces the institution: training, records, property, ritual, habit, sanctions, incentives, family ties, professional identity, or control of information. Then apply pressure—a lost leader, new technology, war, migration, scandal, shortage, strike, schism, or legal change.

Trace what survives, what adapts, who protects the old arrangement, and who uses the disruption to renegotiate it. Avoid assuming that all change is gradual progress or sudden revolution.

## Connect institutions

Link the institution to at least two others. A military depends on taxation, supply, recruitment, medicine, and family arrangements. A school may distribute credentials, reproduce status, challenge inherited privilege, and supply workers to other bodies.

Look for cooperation, jurisdictional overlap, resource competition, and contradictory demands. Institutions need not fit together neatly.

For empires, factions, governments, and revolutionary movements, separate
territorial reach from ideological unity. Map internal blocs, regional
interests, beneficiaries, dissenters, ordinary compromises, and the work that
keeps the system functioning. A state can be oppressive in one domain,
protective in another, and contested by people who disagree about both.

## Audit the design

Check that:

- the institution is made of people and repeated practices, not an abstract name;
- continued existence is explained without claiming the institution is necessary or beneficial to everyone;
- official ideals, actual outcomes, and individual behaviour remain distinct;
- no group is treated as uniformly obedient, corrupt, enlightened, or opposed;
- informal and stateless forms of coordination remain possible;
- player characters may comply, exploit, evade, resist, reform, or leave when the fiction permits;
- darkness and coercion can remain in the setting without becoming the narrator's moral approval.

## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Preserve the setting's internal rules, causal consequences, knowledge boundaries, and player sovereignty. Do not turn fictional darkness, eroticism, coercion, obsession, tragedy, comedy, surrealism, or meta-fiction into a real-world manual or an automatic healthy outcome.
