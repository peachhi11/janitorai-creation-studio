# Design character nonverbal patterns

Build a small, conditional behavioural system rather than a list of emotion gestures.

**Action:** Establish baseline, meaningful variation, function, source,
relationship-specific signature, and recovery.

**Key point:** A gesture is evidence of behaviour, not a universal translation
of emotion or consent.

**Why:** Conditional embodiment keeps subtext legible while preserving
ambiguity and agency.

## Narrative intention and fictional genre logic

Use nonverbal behaviour according to the work's genre and point-of-view rules.
It may support seduction, menace, manipulation, horror, comedy, or surreal
effects, but it must not become universal mind-reading or a shortcut to consent.

## Establish the ordinary baseline

Define the character's usual range in relevant channels:

- facial animation and gaze;
- posture, orientation, movement, and stillness;
- hands, feet, repetitive movement, and self-regulation;
- preferred distance, barriers, territory, and touch;
- pitch, pace, volume, rhythm, breath, pauses, and silence;
- dress, grooming, adornment, carried objects, and spatial arrangement.

Account for culture, family, occupation, age, disability, neurodivergence, pain, medication, sensory needs, mobility, and habit. Do not define one dominant social style as the normal body.

## Map meaningful variation

Choose only variations the fiction will use:

- confidence versus uncertainty;
- ordinary strain, acute danger, anger, attraction, intimacy, exhaustion, and grief;
- public performance versus private release;
- higher, equal, and lower power positions;
- familiar, desired, distrusted, feared, and unknown people;
- being watched versus believing they are alone.

A change from this character's baseline is evidence that something changed. It does not reveal one certain cause.

## Separate source, function, and display

For each recurring behaviour, distinguish:

- what bodily or situational condition produces it;
- whether it expresses, regulates, conceals, performs, or communicates;
- whether it is deliberate, automatic, habitual, or mixed;
- what the character thinks it communicates;
- how different observers interpret it;
- what it actually changes in the interaction.

Allow one behaviour to serve different functions in different contexts. Avoid one gesture per emotion.

## Build relationship-specific signatures

Show how the pattern changes with particular people. Track approach, withdrawal, orientation, interruption, shared rhythm, touch initiation and response, tolerated distance, object placement, and recovery after contact. Reciprocity may grow, fail, become strategic, or remain asymmetrical.

Never infer attraction or consent from gaze, proximity, mirroring, nervousness, stillness, or touch alone. Preserve the other character's agency and leave the player's body and internal state undescribed unless the user provided them.

## Preserve contradiction and change

Include:

- a cue others commonly misread;
- a deliberate performance that sometimes fails;
- a stress behaviour that conflicts with the desired image;
- a setting where the usual pattern reverses;
- a plausible change caused by trust, practice, injury, status, concealment, or deterioration.

Keep the signature recognisable without making it mechanical.

## Deliver the pattern sheet

Return:

- ordinary baseline by selected channel;
- context and relationship variations;
- expression, regulation, masking, and performance functions;
- common observer interpretations and likely errors;
- public/private contradiction;
- scene-ready examples and continuity anchors.
