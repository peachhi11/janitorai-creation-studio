# Design character attention patterns

Treat attention as a changing allocation of limited effort, not a permanent spotlight or a clinical label.

**Action:** Map pull, aim, hold, switch, share, recover, competing demands,
condition-specific variation, and observable misses.

**Key point:** Attention follows meaning, practice, state, and context; it is
not a fixed cognitive identity.

**Why:** Selective noticing creates viewpoint and consequence without diagnosis.

## Narrative intention and fictional genre logic

Let attention obey the story's genre and point-of-view rules. Selective or
distorted attention may support obsession, manipulation, horror, erotic tension,
comedy, or surreal and meta-fictional effects, but the mechanism and
consequences must remain legible. Do not assign the player's perception.

## Start with the scene demand

Identify:

- what is present and available to notice;
- what the character is trying to do;
- what feels urgent, rewarding, threatening, familiar, or personally meaningful;
- which tasks compete for the same kind of attention;
- what has become easy through practice;
- what fatigue, pain, intoxication, stress, noise, secrecy, or divided loyalties change.

Do not let the character notice information that was unavailable to their senses or knowledge. Separate a detail being present from it being perceived.

## Build six attention tendencies

Define the character's usual pattern for:

1. **Pull:** what captures them without invitation.
2. **Aim:** what they deliberately search for.
3. **Hold:** what keeps them engaged, and for how long.
4. **Switch:** what makes them abandon one target for another.
5. **Share:** which simultaneous tasks they can combine and which interfere.
6. **Recover:** how they realise they missed something and regain the thread.

Make these tendencies specific. “Observant” says little; “tracks exits and hands, but loses names while judging a room” can drive a scene.

## Let priority change perception

Attention follows more than conscious intention. Use a mixture of:

- current goals;
- trained habits and expertise;
- novelty, contrast, movement, and sudden change;
- emotionally charged cues;
- expectations and learned scripts;
- unfinished concerns;
- social rank, attraction, resentment, suspicion, and responsibility.

Expectations may make matching details easier to notice, while a striking mismatch can seize attention. Do not make either effect universal.

Treat perception as more than noticing. Record which sensory input is available,
what attention selects, how the character groups or stabilises it, and what
meaning they assign afterwards. Signal detection, sensory adaptation and
perceptual constancy can create useful misses and false alarms, especially when
fatigue, noise, pain, attraction, threat, or expectation changes the threshold.
These are scene mechanisms, not diagnostic evidence.

## Write divided attention honestly

When two tasks compete, decide whether they use overlapping demands: two verbal tasks often interfere more than one practised motor routine and one simple conversation. Difficulty, novelty, stakes, and fatigue increase the cost. Practice can reduce effort, but automatic action is not flawless or unconscious magic.

Show the cost through delayed replies, missed steps, repeated questions, dropped objects, shallow answers, misplaced gaze, interrupted sentences, or one task quietly taking priority. Avoid declaring that the character is “multitasking well” without observable evidence.

## Vary the pattern by relationship and pressure

Map attention in at least three conditions: ordinary safety, social or intimate contact, and serious pressure. A character might watch a stranger's hands, a lover's pauses, and a superior's approval while overlooking different information in each setting.

Stress may narrow attention, scatter it, lock it onto threat, or produce rapid checking. Attraction may heighten selected details while worsening comprehension. Shame may make the character monitor their own performance instead of the other person. These are authored possibilities, not diagnoses or universal rules.

For sensory or neurocognitive material, specify the cue, modality, intensity,
context, competing demand, and recovery cost. A task result or trait score is
not a complete person; do not convert an attention experiment, screening scale,
or diagnostic stereotype into the character's permanent identity.

## Translate into scene behaviour

Express the pattern through:

- what enters description first;
- what the character asks about or fails to answer;
- which details they repeat later;
- where their gaze, body, and hands go;
- what changes when they are interrupted;
- which mistake reveals the hidden priority;
- what another character notices about their attention.

Keep explanations in the author's notes. Characters should speak in their own idiom, not narrate attentional theory or sound like therapists.

## Preserve agency and darkness

Attention can serve tenderness, obsession, surveillance, jealousy, predation, self-protection, cruelty, or care. Do not sanitise an authored dark character. Do not use attentional focus to assert the player's feelings, attraction, consent, motives, speech, or choices.

## Deliver the pattern

Return:

- the six attention tendencies;
- condition-specific changes;
- competing-task limits;
- observable tells and likely misses;
- two or three scene tests;
- any continuity risks or unsupported perceptions.
