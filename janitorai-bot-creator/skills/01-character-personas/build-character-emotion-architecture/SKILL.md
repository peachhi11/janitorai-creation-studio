# Build character emotion architecture

Treat emotion as a changing interaction between stimulus, meaning, body, history, relationship, and choice. Do not reduce a character to one emotional trait.

**Action:** Map cue, appraisal, body, competing feelings, urge, choice, and
aftermath.

**Key point:** Psychological legibility is not emotional neatness or compulsory
regulation.

**Why:** Mixed states and failed coping create character-specific motion without
turning prose into a case report.

## Narrative intention and fictional genre logic

Preserve the story's chosen causal and tonal rules. A dark, coercive, erotic,
tragic, absurd, or morally compromised emotional pattern is not a defect to
correct merely because it is not a healthy real-world model. Keep stimulus,
meaning, choice, and consequence legible, and do not write the player's inner
state or response.

## Map the episode

Record:

- observable trigger or imagined cue;
- immediate appraisal: what does this mean to the character?
- remembered pattern, need, value, or ego investment involved;
- bodily activation and attention shift;
- action urge and competing urge;
- emotion words the character would use versus what an outside observer might call it;
- chosen behaviour, inhibited behaviour, and later residue.

The same event can produce different emotions because people assign different meanings. Trait vulnerability may change the threshold or intensity, but it does not dictate the episode or diagnose the person.

## Keep the emotion pipeline visible

When the source material is useful, separate **sensation → attention → appraisal
→ physiological state → expression/action tendency → interpretation**. A body
change is evidence of activation, not a guaranteed emotion label; an emotion
label is not proof of motive, consent, or truth. Let sleep, pain, sensory load,
expectation, culture, memory, and social context alter the pipeline without
turning any one factor into destiny.

## Build mixed states

Allow anger with fear, desire with shame, relief with grief, pride with envy, tenderness with resentment, or calm that is actually exhaustion. Show which feeling leads, which is concealed, and which becomes visible only later.

## Track regulation

Distinguish immediate intuitive appraisal from deliberate reappraisal. Regulation can involve naming, reframing, delay, movement, humour, withdrawal, connection, ritual, suppression, dissociation, retaliation, or asking for help. Each strategy should have a short-term function and a longer-term cost.

## Track interpersonal regulation

Some characters regulate through other people rather than alone. Specify what
they seek from contact: reassurance, witness, distraction, permission,
provocation, punishment, touch, sex, information, rescue, submission, or proof
of importance. Then show what they do when the response is delayed, refused,
ambiguous, or unexpectedly generous.

Treat this as a relational strategy, not a diagnosis or a claim that the other
person is responsible for stabilising them. It can be tender, manipulative,
mutual, coercive, eroticised, or habitual. The same request may feel like
intimacy to one character and pressure to another.

## Model arousal windows and feedback

Give the character a workable range in which activation sharpens attention and
action. Below it they may detach or delay; above it they may narrow attention,
freeze, flee, lash out, or rely on a safety ritual. Do not make high arousal a
permanent state.

Track reciprocal loops when body and emotion reinforce each other: pain can cue
threat memory, threat can increase bodily pain, poor sleep can lower tolerance
for panic, and avoidance can briefly soothe while preserving fear. These are
story mechanisms, not diagnoses.

## Deliverable

Return an emotion map: cue, meaning, body, competing feelings, urge, regulation attempt, observable behaviour, relationship effect, and delayed aftermath.
