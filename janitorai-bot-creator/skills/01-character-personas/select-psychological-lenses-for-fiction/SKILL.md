# Select psychological lenses for fiction

Choose the smallest set of lenses that clarifies the writing problem. Do not turn a character profile into a survey course.

**Action:** Define the target, select a primary lens and counter-lens, label the
claim status, and translate only supported implications into scenes.

**Key point:** A lens asks a question; it does not reveal a character's hidden
truth or dictate the player's response.

**Why:** Bounded theory keeps psychological language useful without turning
fiction into a case formulation.

## Narrative intention and fictional genre logic

Psychological lenses serve narrative intention rather than replacing it. Use
them to clarify how a dark, manipulative, coercive, erotic, tragic, absurd, or
surreal character operates; do not sanitise the premise, diagnose the player,
or force a therapeutic interpretation onto the fiction.

## Define the question first

Name the exact target: a repeated action, decision, relationship pattern, bodily state, belief, conflict, or change. Specify who does what, in which situations, with what variation and consequences.

Separate five questions that theories often blur:

- What does the character do?
- What keeps it happening now?
- How might it have developed?
- What does it mean to the character?
- What moral, legal, or narrative judgement does the story make?

Psychological explanation does not settle moral or legal responsibility.

## Match lenses to jobs

Use:

- **Behavioural and learning** for antecedents, consequences, reinforcement, punishment, practice, avoidance, and observable change.
- **Cognitive** for attention, memory, appraisal, expectations, categories, problem-solving, and interpretation.
- **Psychodynamic** for hypothesised conflict, defence, recurring relationship meaning, and motives outside awareness. Treat these as interpretations, not discovered facts.
- **Humanistic or existential** for lived experience, meaning, choice, responsibility, aspiration, and conflict between actual and desired self.
- **Biological** for sleep, pain, illness, intoxication, hormones, sensory access, arousal, nervous-system constraints, and possible predispositions. Avoid biological destiny.
- **Evolutionary** for cautious hypotheses about broad functions and trade-offs. Require alternatives and avoid stories that cannot be tested.
- **Trait** for relatively stable individual tendencies across time and context.
- **Sociocultural and ecological** for roles, language, institutions, class, culture, power, community, and historical setting.

Use specialist character skills when the problem belongs squarely to personality, belief, emotion, attachment, cognition, development, communication, or motivation.

## Add a counter-lens

Choose one primary lens and, when the issue is consequential, one counter-lens that could reveal what the first leaves out. A reinforcement account may need meaning and power; a biological account may need situation and culture; a psychodynamic reading may need observable alternatives.

Do not combine theories by pretending they use identical concepts. State whether they compete, complement one another, or answer different levels of the question.

## Mark claim status

Label each conclusion as:

- established story fact;
- direct observation from the text;
- character belief or claim;
- theory-guided interpretation;
- speculative possibility;
- historically influential but disputed model.

Keep teaching summaries, popular articles, assessments, and research evidence distinct.

## Use a mixed-evidence corpus carefully

When a project supplies a large research library, treat its summaries as a
navigation layer, not as proof that every source was closely verified. Preserve
the source's evidence label and provenance. Separate empirical findings,
clinical interpretation, historical theory, practical advice, popular
psychology, game material, and genre convention before combining them.

If several weak or derivative sources repeat a claim, repetition is not
independent confirmation. Use the claim as a character belief, trope shorthand,
or hypothesis unless stronger support and scene evidence justify more.

## Route reference layers without flattening them

When a corpus supplies indexes, glossaries, frameworks, process maps, and
technique libraries, use them in order:

1. **Index:** locate the relevant source or mechanism.
2. **Source claim:** state what the source actually supports and how strong it
   is.
3. **Mechanism:** describe the observable process without importing the source's
   label as a diagnosis or universal law.
4. **Fictional translation:** choose scene behaviour, interpretation, stakes,
   and consequence.
5. **Narrative classification:** decide whether the story presents it as
   mutual, pressured, coercive, mistaken, symbolic, or unresolved.

Tags and glossary entries aid retrieval; they are not conclusions. A framework
is a reusable hypothesis about sequence, not evidence that every character will
follow it.

Treat diagnostic-looking PDFs, worksheets, JSON profiles, and psychometric
reports as external artefacts with an author, purpose, access boundary and
measurement error. Route their claims through the character's belief, the
observer's bias, the relationship system, and current context before using them
to generate behaviour. Do not inject the full schema as hidden objective truth.

## Translate lenses into writing value

For every selected lens, give only:

- the question it asks;
- the evidence it would need;
- one or two scene implications;
- what it cannot establish.

Characters should not recite theoretical labels or sound like therapists unless that language fits their role. Preserve fictional darkness and ambiguity. Never use a lens to dictate the player's feelings, attraction, consent, memories, motives, speech, or choices.

## Deliver the lens plan

Return:

- the defined target;
- primary and counter-lenses with reasons;
- claim-status labels;
- scene-level implications;
- limitations and specialist skills to invoke next.
