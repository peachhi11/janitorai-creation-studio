# Build character motivation architecture

Treat motivation as a changing relation between a person, a situation, an anticipated outcome, and the meaning that outcome has for them. Do not reduce it to one need or a rigid hierarchy.

**Action:** Map direction, intensity, persistence, expected outcome, conflict,
and the meaning attached to the pursuit.

**Key point:** Contradictory motives can coexist without being resolved into a
single healthy priority.

**Why:** Motivation becomes dramatic when it creates choices, costs, and
reversals rather than a label that explains everything.

## Narrative intention and fictional genre logic

Motives belong to the story's internal logic, not a universal moral template.
Keep dark, manipulative, coercive, erotic, tragic, absurd, or self-destructive
motives available when they serve the premise. Trace their causes, choices, and
costs without sanitising them or assigning the player's response.

## Map the layers

For each important pursuit, record:

- immediate want and longer-term aim;
- bodily need, learned drive, incentive, curiosity, pleasure, fear, duty, or belonging need;
- intrinsic value of the activity versus its instrumental reward;
- expected outcome, perceived probability, and value of that outcome;
- what the character believes they must do to get it;
- competing motives and the cost of choosing one;
- context: audience, authority, scarcity, opportunity, relationship, culture, and time pressure.

Use expectancy, instrumentality, and valence as a flexible lens: _Can I achieve it? Will that achievement produce the result? Do I value that result?_ Treat all three as the character’s perception, not objective arithmetic.

For each active motive, also track three separate dials:

- **direction** — what the character moves toward or away from;
- **intensity** — how much attention, risk, or sacrifice it can command;
- **persistence** — how long it survives delay, failure, exhaustion, or a changed audience.

These dials can move independently. A character may still want belonging (direction) while lacking the energy to pursue it (intensity), or keep returning to an old ambition (persistence) after its meaning has thinned out.

Use broad motive families as prompts rather than a ladder: bodily or primary needs, curiosity and activity, affection and affiliation, security, achievement, power, and status. Culture, learning, opportunity, and the current scene shape how any family appears. Never treat a category as destiny or assume one motive explains every choice.

## Track autonomy and control

Distinguish action that feels chosen and integrated from action driven by threat, reward, shame, obligation, internalised approval, or defiance. Compliance and rebellion can both be reactions to control. A character may pursue the same goal autonomously in one setting and under pressure in another.

Show how autonomy, competence, and relatedness support or frustrate one another. Autonomy is not isolation, selfishness, or unlimited freedom; a character can choose responsibility and remain connected to others.

## Build conflict

Give each major motive:

- a trigger;
- a preferred strategy;
- a short-term payoff;
- a long-term cost;
- a failure mode;
- a point where the character can revise, double down, or reinterpret the goal.

Let multiple motives operate at once. A character may want safety and recognition, freedom and belonging, mastery and rest, love and control. Contradiction should create decisions, not disappear into a label.

## Deliverable

Return a motivation map: motive, meaning, expected outcome, context, strategy, conflict, cost, observable behaviour, and conditions for change.
