# Build culturally situated characters

Let culture shape what a character has learned to notice, expect, perform, value, question, and conceal. Do not use it to assign a fixed temperament or destiny.

**Action:** Select the pressures that matter in this story, build the character's lived learning history, and show its effects through choices, speech, and relationships.
**Key point:** Cultural context is a force acting through a person, not a substitute for personhood.
**Why:** Overlapping memberships and individual responses prevent stereotype while keeping culture narratively consequential.

## Narrative intention and fictional genre logic

Let culture operate through the story's established social and genre rules.
Preserve conflict, taboo, coercion, eroticism, or difference when narratively
intended without treating a fictional society as a real-world prescription or
sanitising it into a single acceptable model.

## Map overlapping memberships

Identify the contexts that matter to this character:

- household and kin;
- neighbourhood, region, and nation;
- language and dialect communities;
- faith, ritual, and moral traditions;
- class, education, occupation, and institution;
- generation, gender, sexuality, disability, and life stage;
- migration, diaspora, minority, mixed, or convert experience;
- chosen communities, fandoms, scenes, and political groups.

Choose the few that exert real pressure in the story. Avoid treating identity as a checklist of demographic facts.

## Build a learning history

For each relevant context, ask:

1. Who taught the character its rules and stories?
2. Which lessons were explicit, and which were absorbed through reaction and routine?
3. Where did the character fit easily, and where were they corrected or excluded?
4. Which practices are habitual, strategic, cherished, resented, misunderstood, or unknown?
5. What changed after travel, education, loss, conversion, success, discrimination, or a new relationship?

Culture becomes character-specific through experience, not by copying a group average.

## Translate context into behaviour

Show cultural learning through:

- what counts as polite, intimate, boastful, childish, serious, or dangerous in this relationship;
- whom the character may question, touch, invite, interrupt, refuse, or address informally;
- how they handle hospitality, debt, privacy, grief, celebration, work, courtship, conflict, and repair;
- which languages, registers, jokes, references, and silences they use with different people;
- what they assume needs no explanation;
- which rule they follow in public and violate in private.

Vary behaviour by audience, stakes, place, and power. Adaptation or code-switching is not proof that one version is false.

## Preserve individual difference

Add at least one point of alignment, one point of friction, and one area of uncertainty between the character and each major community. Give the character peers from the same context who make different choices for intelligible reasons.

Separate:

- shared knowledge from personal belief;
- expected conduct from actual conduct;
- public performance from private habit;
- cultural pressure from personality, desire, fear, and material need;
- how outsiders label the character from how the character understands themself.

## Write voice without performance for outsiders

Use vocabulary, rhythm, reference, and conversational habits that arise from the character's actual languages, relationships, and work. Do not make them continually explain their background, translate ordinary life for the reader, or speak as a representative of everyone like them.

Accent and second-language features require specific linguistic research. Avoid decorative misspelling, interchangeable “foreign” syntax, and perfect consistency that ignores fatigue, familiarity, audience, or practice.

## Protect contradiction and agency

A character may love a community and resent its demands, reject a norm while using its language, defend a practice they privately avoid, or misremember their own history. They may be cruel, generous, conforming, rebellious, devout, cynical, assimilated, traditional, or undecided without any stance becoming the culture's official truth.

For player characters, provide reasonable background knowledge and social consequences without deciding their identity, loyalty, attraction, consent, feelings, or choices.

When disability, neurodivergence, gender, sexuality, migration, or class
intersects with culture, show the actual setting that changes the choice:
family duty, language access, diagnosis, stigma, transport, money, work,
school, healthcare, or community safety. Do not turn intersectionality into a
stack of labels or assume that one identity explains the others.

## Audit the character

- Remove any trait justified only by nationality, race, ethnicity, faith, or culture.
- Check that hardship is not the character's only cultural content.
- Check that food, clothing, accent, and ritual are connected to relationships and meaning rather than decorative difference.
- Include ordinary competence, humour, boredom, taste, work, and private preference.
- Preserve fictional darkness and conflict without treating prejudice or coercion as the natural essence of a people.
