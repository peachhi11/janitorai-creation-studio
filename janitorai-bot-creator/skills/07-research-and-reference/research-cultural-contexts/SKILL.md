# Research cultural contexts

Research culture as a changing field of shared meanings, practices, institutions, histories, and disagreements. Do not treat it as a personality assigned to everyone in a country.

**Action:** Define the smallest useful context, gather inside and comparative
views, track variation and provenance, and mark invention separately.

**Key point:** Cultural context shapes available meanings and pressures; it
does not determine an individual character.

**Why:** Situated research prevents stereotype while giving scenes specific
social consequences.

## Define the actual context

Replace a broad label with the smallest useful unit. Specify:

- place, period, and political setting;
- community, language, faith, class, occupation, generation, or subculture;
- the practice or question being researched;
- whose viewpoint the story follows;
- contact with other communities, migration, colonisation, conflict, trade, or diaspora;
- what must be accurate and what the author intends to invent.

Culture is not interchangeable with nationality, race, ethnicity, or religion. These may overlap without producing one uniform group.

## Use inside and comparative views

Combine two lenses:

- **inside view:** local categories, meanings, disagreements, histories, and reasons people give for their own practices;
- **comparative view:** patterns that help compare contexts without assuming one culture supplies the neutral standard.

If a concept was developed elsewhere, check whether its wording, measurement, and social meaning travel. A familiar label may conceal different practices, while two different labels may serve similar purposes.

## Build a source set

Prefer a mixture of:

- work by members of the community, including people who disagree with one another;
- historical or institutional records appropriate to the period;
- careful scholarship that states its population and limits;
- firsthand accounts situated by class, region, gender, age, faith, occupation, and relationship to power;
- current sources when the setting or living practice can change.

Record who produced each source, when, for whom, and under what conditions. Do not count repeated summaries of the same study as independent support.

## Separate kinds of claim

Mark each finding as one of:

- documented practice or institution;
- reported norm or ideal;
- observed tendency in a defined sample;
- contested interpretation;
- individual testimony;
- outsider description;
- popular shorthand;
- creative invention.

Avoid turning an average difference into a rule about an individual. Check overlap within groups, exceptions, regional and generational change, and the effect of situation.

## Examine power and contact

Ask whose behaviour was recorded, who had the authority to name it, and who was absent. Check whether the source treats urban, educated, wealthy, Western, majority, male, or institutional participants as the human default.

Include the effects of law, empire, missionisation, schooling, media, tourism, migration, economic pressure, and unequal translation when relevant. Cultural exchange is rarely a meeting between sealed, equal containers.

## Turn research into fiction guidance

Extract material that can affect action:

- what people learn and from whom;
- what varies by relationship or setting;
- what is expected, admired, mocked, feared, hidden, or disputed;
- what resources and institutions sustain a practice;
- how insiders explain it and how outsiders may misread it;
- what has changed within living memory.

Label uncertain or invented material honestly. Do not claim authenticity from a short checklist, a name list, or one cultural consultant.

## Audit the result

Check that the guidance does not:

- describe a whole people with one trait, value, family form, gesture, or communication style;
- use Western experience or the researcher's home culture as an invisible baseline;
- mistake difference for deficiency or novelty for timeless tradition;
- treat living sacred material as free decorative texture;
- erase mixed, migrant, diasporic, multilingual, dissenting, or marginal members;
- require the story to become an educational report rather than fiction.

## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Preserve the setting's internal rules, causal consequences, knowledge boundaries, and player sovereignty. Do not turn fictional darkness, eroticism, coercion, obsession, tragedy, comedy, surrealism, or meta-fiction into a real-world manual or an automatic healthy outcome.
