# Catalogue source provenance and evidence

Build a map from source to claim, not a pile of titles or an authority-shaped
list of links.

**Action:** Identify the source, verify its provenance, classify its claim type
and evidence tier, record limits and conflicts, then route only supported claims
into the creative task.

**Key point:** A source can be useful without being strong evidence, and a
strong study may not answer the fictional question being asked.

**Why:** Provenance and scope prevent research theatre, citation laundering, and
psychological or cultural overclaiming.

## Create the source record

Record only what can be verified:

- stable source identifier, title, creator, date, edition, publisher, and URL or
  archive location where available;
- source form: primary research, review, historical record, theory, textbook,
  practical guide, popular interpretation, fictional exemplar, worksheet, or
  generated note;
- population, setting, method, sample, time period, and language where relevant;
- extracted claim, exact location, confidence, and citation-ready reference;
- access status: readable, partial, OCR-derived, metadata-only, or unavailable;
- intended use: evidence, historical context, craft lens, example, hypothesis,
  or discard.

Do not fill missing metadata with guesses. Mark unknowns explicitly.

## Tier claims, not prestige

Use a practical tier such as:

1. **Direct evidence:** the source observes, measures, records, or argues the
   claim within a stated scope.
2. **Supported synthesis:** a review or multiple compatible sources support a
   bounded conclusion.
3. **Situated interpretation:** a credible theoretical, historical, or insider
   interpretation whose limits must remain visible.
4. **Craft reference:** useful technique, example, or vocabulary that does not
   establish how people generally behave.
5. **Popular shorthand or hypothesis:** usable as a character belief or prompt
   lens, not as an authorial law.

Repeated copies, derivative summaries, search snippets, and citations of one
original source do not raise the tier.

## Route claims into fiction

For each adopted claim, state:

- what it can change in a character, scene, relationship, setting, or system;
- what it cannot establish about an individual;
- relevant variation, contradiction, and uncertainty;
- whether it belongs to world truth, character belief, diegetic artefact, author
  hypothesis, or fictional genre logic.

Keep diagnostic-looking forms, popular typologies, erotic manuals, and craft
examples as artefacts or lenses unless the narrative independently establishes
their claims. Do not make characters sound like research reports.

## Audit citations

Check that every citation points to the source actually consulted, that quoted
language is marked, that paraphrase preserves scope, and that a claim is not
stronger than its evidence. Record conflicts instead of silently selecting the
most convenient source.

## Deliverable

Return a source catalogue, evidence-tier table, claim ledger, conflicts and gaps,
citation-ready records, and a fiction-routing note for each adopted claim.
