# Historicise sexuality in fiction

Reconstruct what an experience could mean to people in that time, not merely whether the act existed.

**Action:** Anchor the period's words, institutions, material conditions, and competing interpretations before assigning a character a modern category.
**Key point:** Acts, identities, roles, and meanings do not arrive as one package.
**Why:** Historical specificity prevents present-day assumptions from erasing ambiguity, danger, pleasure, or difference.

## Anchor the setting

Identify the date range, location, class, religion, legal jurisdiction, medical tradition, available technology, and communities relevant to the character. Avoid century-wide or civilisation-wide generalisations.

## Separate act, category, and identity

A behaviour may exist before, after, or outside the identity categories now associated with it. Determine:

- available words and classifications;
- whether conduct is understood as sin, crime, illness, vice, habit, role, status, relationship, temperament, or identity;
- whether the character recognises a stable kind of person or only particular acts and circumstances;
- how labels differ between authorities, communities, lovers, enemies, and private thought.

Do not simply replace a modern word with an antique synonym while keeping modern assumptions intact.

## Reconstruct material conditions

Account for privacy, architecture, clothing, contraception, reproduction, disease knowledge, transport, communication, policing, print, images, commerce, and household structure. These conditions shape opportunity, evidence, risk, secrecy, and what characters can know.

## Use evidence carefully

Distinguish:

- contemporary records from later interpretation;
- elite prescription from common practice;
- prosecution from prevalence;
- medical classification from lived identity;
- literary fantasy from ordinary conduct;
- silence from absence.

Mark uncertainty. A sensational case, encyclopaedia entry, artwork, joke, or moral warning proves circulation of an idea, not typical behaviour.

## Keep characters human

Avoid both period caricature and suspiciously modern lectures. Characters can resist their culture without possessing twenty-first-century vocabulary. Give them plausible desires, rationalisations, compromises, coded speech, practical knowledge, and contradictions.

## Track historical change

Show how words, institutions, and meanings shift across a lifetime or between generations. Reform does not move evenly; law, medicine, religion, art, community, and private practice may change at different speeds.

## Deliver the historical frame

Return:

- available categories and contested terms;
- governing institutions and material constraints;
- public ideals versus probable practices;
- character-specific knowledge and blind spots;
- anachronism risks;
- evidence confidence and unresolved questions.

Do not use dated medical or sexological claims as current truth, romanticise repression, or sanitise historically plausible prejudice and harm.

## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Preserve the setting's internal rules, causal consequences, knowledge boundaries, and player sovereignty. Do not turn fictional darkness, eroticism, coercion, obsession, tragedy, comedy, surrealism, or meta-fiction into a real-world manual or an automatic healthy outcome.
