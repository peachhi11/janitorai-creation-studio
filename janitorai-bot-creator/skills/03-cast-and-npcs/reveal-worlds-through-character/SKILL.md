# Reveal worlds through character

Show the world as lived knowledge: partial, practical, emotional, interested, and sometimes wrong. A character rarely explains what is ordinary to them unless the situation gives them a reason.

**Action:** Filter setting evidence through need, viewpoint, knowledge, status,
emotion, and what the character chooses to conceal.

**Key point:** Discovery can remain partial, mistaken, or contested; lore does
not need to arrive as a lecture.

**Why:** Character-filtered worldbuilding makes setting information affect the
scene instead of stopping it.

## Set the viewpoint filter

Before writing the scene, note:

- what the character needs right now;
- what they notice through work, class, body, upbringing, loyalties, and fear;
- what feels ordinary, prestigious, shameful, sacred, irritating, or invisible to them;
- what they know firsthand, know by reputation, or misunderstand;
- what they would conceal, soften, exaggerate, or refuse to discuss.

Two characters in the same street should not receive the same version of it.

## Choose evidence with a job to do

Reveal setting through details that affect the present action:

- a door the character may not use;
- a tool worn smooth by repeated work;
- clothing adjusted for weather, rank, modesty, or danger;
- food stretched, shared, refused, or displayed;
- a form of address that grants or withholds respect;
- a route chosen to avoid cost, surveillance, memory, or obligation;
- a rule enforced unevenly;
- a familiar object used in an unfamiliar way.

Prefer a few connected details over a catalogue. Let their meaning accumulate across scenes.

## Give exposition a motive

When information must be spoken, give the speaker a reason: instructing, warning, bargaining, boasting, teasing, lying, remembering, translating, testing, or excluding someone.

Keep the wording local to the character's life. People may speak in trade terms, family sayings, insults, prayers, jokes, rumours, and practical comparisons. They should not sound like therapists, sociologists, or guidebooks unless that is genuinely their role and voice.

Avoid exchanges in which one local asks another to recite facts they both know. Use disagreement about meaning, consequences, exceptions, or what to do next.

## Preserve knowledge boundaries

Track separately:

- what the setting establishes as true;
- what a community commonly believes;
- what this character believes;
- what the reader or player has learned.

Do not let every character know hidden history, private motives, or system rules. When a player character would reasonably know a custom or danger, provide that information before the choice rather than manufacturing a trap from missing context.

## Let behaviour carry culture

Translate a world rule into action. If hospitality is binding, show who serves, who eats first, what accepting food commits someone to, and how a reluctant host creates an exception. If magic is licensed, show the queue, fee, forged mark, professional habit, or fear of inspection.

Allow variation. A person can comply publicly and mock privately, cherish one tradition and reject another, misunderstand their own history, or behave differently with family, authorities, rivals, and strangers.

## Manage disclosure

Reveal what the scene needs for comprehension and choice. Delay information when a viewpoint character lacks it or suspense benefits, but do not hide facts solely to force a mistake.

After a draft, mark each explanatory passage and ask:

1. Does someone need this now?
2. Does it change a choice, relationship, expectation, or consequence?
3. Could action, an object, a reaction, or a shorter line carry it?
4. Is the voice recognisably this character's?

Cut, relocate, or reshape anything that has no present dramatic function.

## Protect character and player freedom

- Describe pressures and likely knowledge without dictating a player character's feelings, attraction, consent, beliefs, or decisions.
- Keep dark customs, coercive institutions, prejudice, and violence inside the fiction; do not turn them into authorial approval or an excuse for every member of a society.
- Let characters be implicated, contradictory, ignorant, cruel, generous, or self-deceiving without diagnosing them in narrative shorthand.
- Preserve room for discovery, resistance, complicity, change, and unintended consequences.

## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Dark, erotic, coercive, obsessive, tragic, comic, surreal, or unresolved material may be intentional. Preserve causal consequences, character specificity, truth-layer boundaries, and player sovereignty; do not turn a fictional pattern into diagnosis, a healthy-relationship lesson, or a real-world operational manual.
