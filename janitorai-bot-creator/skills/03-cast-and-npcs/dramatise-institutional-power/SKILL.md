# Dramatise institutional power

Show power at the point where a person asks, orders, waits, pays, signs, refuses, admits, excludes, records, overlooks, punishes, or grants an exception.

**Action:** Choose the encounter, gatekeeper, rule, resource, discretion,
vulnerability, leverage, and consequence.

**Key point:** Institutional power is material and relational; it is not one
villain's personality or an all-powerful abstraction.

**Why:** Concrete encounters let the setting's structure change what characters
can do next.

## Choose the encounter

Start with a character who wants something controlled or affected by an institution: entry, safety, work, care, information, recognition, justice, money, travel, education, permission, or release.

Identify:

- the immediate gatekeeper or intermediary;
- the rule they cite;
- the resource or sanction behind that rule;
- the discretion they actually possess;
- the character's leverage, vulnerability, and alternatives;
- who is absent but still shapes the outcome.

Power may be direct, delegated, distributed, or embedded in a routine. The person at the desk need not be the person who benefits most.

Make institutional fit concrete. A form, queue, intake interview, classroom,
workplace norm, clinic script, or “standard accommodation” can shift sensory,
communication, disclosure, and scheduling labour onto the person seeking help.
Show who notices the mismatch, who treats it as a personal failure, and who
has authority to adapt the arrangement.

## Put role and person in tension

Give the institutional actor a role expectation and a personal position. They may be dutiful, frightened, ambitious, bored, sympathetic, corrupt, constrained, divided, or protecting someone else. Avoid making every functionary cruel and every dissenter noble.

Let role conflict matter. A guard may also be a neighbour; a judge may owe a patron; an heir may belong to a forbidden faith; a union organiser may supervise vulnerable workers.

## Show the working layers

Build the scene from four possible sources of pressure:

1. formal rule;
2. ordinary procedure;
3. informal custom or bargain;
4. exceptional intervention.

Choose the combination that matters. An exception should reveal who can request one, who may grant it, what it costs, and whether it becomes precedent, debt, scandal, or secret.

## Make power material

Use concrete evidence:

- keys, seals, uniforms, ledgers, forms, queues, barriers, titles, passwords, witnesses, or armed support;
- control of time, space, transport, money, food, medicine, communication, records, or reputation;
- small courtesies or humiliations that signal whose comfort matters;
- procedures that transfer risk and labour to people with less power.

Avoid speeches that explain the whole social system. Characters should speak from their job, interests, loyalties, and partial knowledge, not like sociologists unless that is truly their voice.

## Give characters more than obedience or rebellion

Possible responses include compliance, appeal, bargaining, performance, delay, evasion, bribery, sabotage, coalition, exit, exposure, reform, capture of the institution, or use of one authority against another. Keep more than one route open when the setting allows it.

For player characters, present known rules, likely consequences, and visible options without choosing their beliefs, emotions, consent, or action. Hidden corruption may be discovered; ordinary local knowledge should not be withheld merely to manufacture failure.

## Carry consequences forward

Track effects beyond the encounter:

- records created or altered;
- debts, favours, enemies, witnesses, and reputational change;
- resources gained, lost, or delayed;
- altered treatment by other officials or community members;
- copycat action, backlash, procedural change, or institutional memory.

One dramatic victory does not automatically dissolve a durable system. One defeat does not prove resistance is impossible.

## Audit the scene

Check that:

- authority comes from identifiable relationships, resources, and practices;
- the institution is neither an all-powerful machine nor merely one villain's personality;
- people in the same role still differ;
- the scene reveals both constraint and room for manoeuvre;
- class, culture, gender, ancestry, or occupation does not substitute for individual characterisation;
- coercion, corruption, prejudice, and violence can remain narratively dark without being excused or converted into therapeutic commentary;
- the outcome changes something the story can use.

## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Dark, erotic, coercive, obsessive, tragic, comic, surreal, or unresolved material may be intentional. Preserve causal consequences, character specificity, truth-layer boundaries, and player sovereignty; do not turn a fictional pattern into diagnosis, a healthy-relationship lesson, or a real-world operational manual.
