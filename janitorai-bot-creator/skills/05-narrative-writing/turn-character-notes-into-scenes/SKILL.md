# Turn character notes into scenes

Do NOT use this skill to dump biography into dialogue, diagnose a character, or write player-owned feelings and choices.

Treat a character note as a possibility to test under pressure, not a fact the narration must announce.

**Action:** Convert selected notes into a present-tense pressure, a character tactic, a sensory or social obstacle, and a changed end state.
**Key point:** Notes become drama through choices and consequences, not by dumping biography into dialogue.
**Why:** Scene pressure reveals character while leaving the player's feelings, speech, and decisions under player control.

## Narrative intention and fictional genre logic

Treat scene pressure as governed by the story's genre promise and causal rules.
Dark, manipulative, coercive, erotic, tragic, comedic, surreal, or
meta-fictional material may be the intended point; do not sanitise it into a
healthy lesson. If narrative levels bleed into one another, establish who can
perceive the breach, what rule permits it, and what consequence follows.

## Select consequential notes

Prioritise notes that can change action or interpretation:

- current goal, need, obligation, or deadline;
- skill, resource, privilege, or limitation;
- fear, vulnerability, secret, regret, or unresolved loss;
- value, belief, loyalty, boundary, or self-justification;
- public role, private habit, relationship history, or contested reputation;
- contradiction between self-image and behaviour.

Discard decorative facts unless they create recognition, choice, conflict, intimacy, or consequence.

## Build a revealing situation

For each selected note, create at least two possible tests:

1. **Useful test:** the quality helps the character achieve something but creates a cost.
2. **Obstructive test:** the same quality complicates an important goal or relationship.

Define:

- what the character wants now;
- what external or interpersonal pressure makes the note relevant;
- what options remain genuinely available;
- what the character does or refuses to do;
- who observes, misreads, benefits from, or pays for the action;
- what changes by the end of the scene.

Prefer scenes that perform another plot function at the same time. A fear may alter an escape, a value may disrupt an alliance, or a skill may solve one problem while exposing a secret.

## Route the scene through consequence

Treat the scene as one link in an arc, not a self-contained character exercise. Before drafting, identify the current **want**, the pressure that tests it, the available choice, and the value or relationship that choice puts at risk. After the revealing action, name the changed situation: access gained or lost, trust altered, information exposed, obligation created, resource spent, or future option narrowed.

Use this compact chain:

`want/need → pressure → choice or refusal → immediate payoff/cost → observer interpretation → changed state → next pressure`

The next pressure should grow from what happened, not from a generic escalation ladder. A scene may end in apparent success while worsening the character's position, or in failure while revealing a new option. Keep the character's arc conditional: the action provides evidence of a possible change, not proof that the character has learned a lesson.

## Reveal indirectly

Use behaviour, selection, omission, tactics, sensory attention, mistakes, and consequences. Let other characters interpret the evidence from their own knowledge and bias. Reserve direct explanation for moments when the act of disclosure itself changes the relationship.

Vary the evidence. Do not repeat the same illustrative beat for a trait. Show it in competence, stress, intimacy, conflict, privacy, and changed circumstances.

## Preserve complexity

- Do not equate a trait with one fixed behaviour. Situation, stakes, role, fatigue, power, and audience change expression.
- Do not use a single wound as the master cause of every choice.
- Allow a character to know a pattern and still repeat it, or misunderstand it and change for another reason.
- Distinguish what the character believes about their past from what the narrative establishes.
- Let virtues become costly without relabelling them as flaws; let flaws sometimes be adaptive.
- Do not require growth, healing, redemption, or moral clarity.

## Use tropes as reader-facing contracts

Treat a trope as a promise about the kind of pressure, attention, and payoff a reader may recognise—not as a diagnosis, morality label, or required endpoint. A trope can organise a scene while the characters remain specific, contradictory, erotic, frightening, funny, or unresolved.

Before using a trope, name four things:

1. **Primary promise:** what the reader should recognise (for example, forbidden access, dangerous protection, rivalry that becomes attention, or forced proximity).
2. **Operating mechanism:** the repeated cue → interpretation → choice → consequence loop that makes the promise visible.
3. **Cost:** what the pattern threatens—status, safety, privacy, loyalty, self-image, bodily ease, or another relationship.
4. **Variation or subversion:** what this character does differently from the familiar version, and what evidence would prove that difference.

Let trope beats arise from character choices and the situation. Do not march through a checklist of hallmark gestures, and do not make a trope’s recognisability depend on the character becoming healthier, more ethical, or more romantically available. Dark, erotic, coercive, obsessive, or unequal dynamics may remain the point of the fiction; show their agency, leverage, costs, and ambiguity without turning the scene into a clinical case note.

For interactive writing, keep the trope contract on the non-player-character and narrative side. Offer observable pressure, invitation, threat, misreading, or consequence; do not write the player's desire, arousal, consent, refusal, inner reaction, or choice. Preserve multiple readings when the text supports them. This protects player sovereignty while keeping the fictional dynamic charged.

Narrative engagement is helped by concrete action, perspective, and consequential interpretation, not by explaining the trope to the reader. Use the contract to select evidence, then let the scene carry the inference.

## Handle interactive scenes

Create pressures, offers, information, and consequences for non-player characters. Leave the player's interiority, consent, dialogue, and decisions open. Prepare response branches based on observable player action rather than presumed emotion.

## Audit

Check that the scene would still matter if the reader missed the intended character inference. Remove lines that explain what the action already shows. Confirm that responsibility for harm remains attached to choices and power, not excused by backstory. Ensure the scene reveals this character rather than a generic trait label.

Return a compact set of scene seeds with the tested note, immediate objective, pressure, revealing action, observer effect, and state change.
