# Write manipulation and counterplay

Portray influence as a fallible interaction, not a command that rewrites another mind.

**Action:** Map goal, leverage, information, attempt, interpretation, resistance,
adaptation, changed options, and residue.

**Key point:** Fictional genre logic can preserve manipulation, coercion,
obsession, or predation without making the tactic infallible or instructional.
Keep the genre's promise and causal rules visible: a tactic may work because
the story establishes leverage, symbolism, or heightened convention, but it
must still produce consequences rather than functioning as a universal trick.

**Why:** Fallibility gives the influenced character memory, counterplay, and
meaningful consequences.

## Ground the attempt

Establish:

- the influencer's goal, leverage, information, and self-justification;
- the other person's needs, alternatives, suspicions, and constraints;
- their relationship history and current power balance;
- audience, setting, time pressure, records, and exit routes;
- what the influencer risks if the tactic fails or is exposed.

Choose behaviour that fits the character's knowledge, patience, status, and style.

Treat coercive-control charts, “manipulation techniques,” and self-help lists
as source artefacts or author-level taxonomies. Abstract them into patterns of
pressure, restricted options, isolation, monitoring, audience effects, and
counterpower. Do not copy them into reusable scripts or assume that a named
tactic has a predictable effect.

## Write the contest loop

Build each turn as:

**Goal → leverage → attempt → interpretation → resistance or compliance → adaptation → changed options → cost and residue.**

For deliberate reality-distortion, track the truth–emotion loop separately: trusted claim or omission → altered belief → emotional disturbance → changed decision → new “evidence” created by that decision. The loop can be interrupted by records, witnesses, competing interpretations, time, or the target’s counterpower. Do not treat confusion as consent, and do not make the tactic infallible.

Compliance may be sincere, strategic, frightened, partial, temporary, or performed. Resistance may be direct, delayed, hidden, collective, self-protective, or impossible at that moment.

## Select tactics at story level

Use broad fictional mechanisms rather than reusable coercive scripts:

- concealment, selective truth, or contradictory accounts;
- flattery, idealisation, strategic usefulness, or belonging;
- withdrawal, scarcity, unpredictability, or intermittent reward;
- humiliation, status challenge, plausible-deniability humour, or audience pressure;
- triangulation, reputation pressure, gatekeeping, or isolation;
- option framing, delay, urgency, surveillance, threat, deprivation, or force.

Show concrete action and consequence. Do not provide embedded-command recipes, neg lists, objection-defeat language, or vulnerability-targeting instructions.

Keep the influence type legible in the scene:

- **conformity:** the character adjusts to a group or perceived group norm;
- **compliance:** the character responds to a request without necessarily
  accepting its underlying position;
- **obedience:** the character follows a recognised authority or command;
- **persuasion:** the character's judged position may move, resist, or become
  more extreme.

Use social-judgement distances as author-level diagnostics: a request near an
anchor may feel ordinary, a marginal request may invite negotiation, and a
rejected position may provoke contrast, defensiveness, mockery, silence, or
counterargument. These are narrative possibilities, not guaranteed tricks.

## Preserve the other character

Give the influenced character perception, priorities, error, adaptation, and memory. They may misunderstand, participate, desire part of the exchange, or make a costly choice without becoming programmable. Do not use attraction, arousal, fear, freezing, appeasement, intoxication, dependency, or previous consent as automatic agreement.

Never author the player's internal state, speech, action, or submission. Write openings, pressures, and consequences that leave the next choice available.

## Track aftermath

Carry forward:

- evidence created or destroyed;
- trust gained, spent, or poisoned;
- new dependency or counterpower;
- witnesses and competing narratives;
- shame, anger, fear, loyalty, resentment, or misattribution when canon supports them;
- escalation risk, exposure, retaliation, departure, or renewed control.

Success should create maintenance costs. Failure should change the relationship.

## Keep dialogue natural

Let characters flatter, joke, imply, deny, delay, threaten, reward, withdraw, recruit, or rewrite events in their own voice. Keep terms such as manipulation, coercion, and gaslighting in author analysis unless the speaker would plausibly use them.

## Deliver the scene pass

Return:

- the revised scene or beat sequence;
- the influence-and-counterplay loop;
- each actor's options and constraints;
- partial success, failure, and exposure points;
- continuity consequences and unresolved risk.
