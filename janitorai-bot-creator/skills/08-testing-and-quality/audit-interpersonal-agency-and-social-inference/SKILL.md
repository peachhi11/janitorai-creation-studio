## Narrative Intention and fictional genre logic

Treat the skill as a tool inside a chosen fictional genre, not a universal moral or psychological law. Dark, erotic, coercive, obsessive, tragic, comic, surreal, or unresolved material may be intentional. Preserve causal consequences, character specificity, truth-layer boundaries, and player sovereignty; do not turn a fictional pattern into diagnosis, a healthy-relationship lesson, or a real-world operational manual.

# Audit interpersonal agency and social inference

Find where an interpretation has quietly become control or narrator fact.

**Action:** Separate observable cue, viewpoint interpretation, character claim,
group script, narrator assertion, and evidence-supported fact.

**Key point:** An interpretation may remain wrong, strategic, or unresolved;
the audit must not turn it into player fact.

**Why:** Distinguishing inference from control preserves social tension and
agency.

## Separate cue from conclusion

Mark:

- observable behaviour;
- viewpoint interpretation;
- character claim;
- cultural or group script;
- narrator assertion;
- evidence-supported story fact.

Flag any single cue treated as proof of motive, honesty, attraction, danger, guilt, or consent. Replace certainty with the smallest justified claim, an alternative explanation, or evidence that genuinely establishes the fact.

## Check person, situation, and culture

Flag stable-trait explanations that ignore audience, role, authority, dependency, material risk, group sanctions, history, or freedom to leave. Check whether a Western, binary-gender, heterosexual, workplace, classroom, or laboratory model has been presented as universal.

Culture should be specific enough to include disagreement, subculture, status, generation, institution, and individual variation. Do not replace one stereotype with another.

## Audit narrative agency

Ask who receives:

- active verbs and goals;
- interiority and interpretation;
- meaningful choices;
- the ability to refuse, leave, revise, or initiate;
- consequences that alter later action.

Words such as target, obstacle, prize, test, conquest, lead, break, overcome, or resistance can reveal an intentional coercive worldview. They may remain in character voice, but the story should not silently turn the other person into an object or make the worldview correct.

## Audit disclosure and consent

Flag:

- penalties, dares, humiliation, persistence, or relationship threats used to force disclosure;
- reciprocity treated as a debt;
- silence, freezing, intoxication, invitation, clothing, nudity, prior intimacy, flirtation, earlier consent, or failure to resist treated as present consent;
- refusal reframed as confusion, coyness, a test, or an obstacle;
- consent to one act treated as consent to another.

Consent is voluntary, specific, informed, ongoing, and revocable. A fictional character may violate it; the audit distinguishes intentional portrayal from accidental endorsement. Never supply the player's consent, attraction, disclosure, thoughts, feelings, speech, or choices.

## Preserve darkness and natural voice

Do not sanitise manipulative, coercive, prejudiced, violent, deceptive, or morally ambiguous fiction. Clarify whose belief and action it is, who bears the consequence, and what the viewpoint can know. Do not solve the problem by making characters speak like counsellors.

## Deliver the audit

Return:

- high-consequence agency or consent defects first;
- cue-versus-inference errors;
- ignored situational and cultural factors;
- intentional in-character framing to retain;
- the smallest revision for each accidental problem;
- player-sovereignty constraints and unresolved uncertainties.
